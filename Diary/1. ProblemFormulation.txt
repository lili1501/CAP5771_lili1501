The objective of this project is to explore early clinical deterioration in ICU patients 
using structured physiologic and laboratory data from the MIMIC-IV database. 
Specifically, we focus on extracting and analyzing the first 72 hours of ICU data to 
understand patterns that may signal worsening organ function.
The problem is measurable because ICU deterioration can be quantified through changes in 
vital signs (heart rate, mean arterial pressure, respiratory rate, oxygenation), laboratory
abnormalities (lactate, creatinine, bilirubin, white blood cell count, platelet count), and 
length of stay characteristics. These variables are consistently recorded in ICU 
environments and are suitable for structured analysis.

A 72-hour window was selected to capture early physiologic instability while minimizing 
data leakage from later outcomes. This time-based restriction ensures that features are 
temporally aligned and reflect early ICU physiology rather than downstream consequences.

The problem is clinically meaningful because early detection of instability may guide
interventions, resource allocation, and severity assessment in critical care settings.


1. What is the central question?
The central question of this project is:
Can early physiologic and laboratory data from the first 72 hours of 
ICU admission be used to characterize and identify patterns of clinical deterioration?

Specifically, the project seeks to determine whether structured ICU measurements 
(vital signs and laboratory values) contain early signals of instability that may 
indicate worsening organ function or severity of illness.

2. Why is it important in context?
Early detection of clinical deterioration in ICU settings is critically 
important because:

ICU patients are physiologically unstable.
Small changes in vital signs or laboratory values may precede major complications.
Early identification allows timely intervention, potentially improving outcomes.
ICU resources are limited and high-cost.

In the broader healthcare context, understanding early deterioration patterns 
supports better triage, monitoring prioritization, and resource allocation in 
critical care environments.

From a data perspective, ICU monitoring systems continuously generate structured 
physiologic data, making this problem well-suited for database-driven analysis.

3. What would “success” look like?
Detecting early signs of possible symptoms.

4. What variables are essential, and Why?
Vital Signs
Heart Rate (HR) – indicator of cardiovascular stress.
Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) – indicator of perfusion and shock.
Respiratory Rate (RR) – marker of respiratory distress.
Oxygen Saturation (SpO₂) – reflects oxygenation status.
Temperature – marker of infection or systemic inflammation.
FiO₂ and PaO₂ – indicators of respiratory failure severity.

Laboratory Values
Lactate – marker of tissue hypoperfusion and shock.
Creatinine – indicator of renal function.
Bilirubin – marker of hepatic dysfunction.
WBC count – marker of infection or inflammation.
Platelet count – indicator of coagulation dysfunction.

Length of Stay (LOS)
Provides contextual severity and helps interpret early physiologic instability.
These variables were selected because they represent core physiologic systems 
(cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, hepatic, hematologic) and are routinely 
measured in ICU care.

5. Who will use the results?
The results may be useful for:
Critical care clinicians interested in early physiologic trends.
Hospital administrators evaluating ICU severity patterns.
Data scientists developing early warning systems.
Healthcare researchers studying organ dysfunction progression.
In this project context, the results primarily support exploratory modeling and 
database-driven ICU research.

6. Is this realistic given constraints?
Yes, this project is realistic given the constraints of:
Using structured MIMIC-IV data.
Restricting analysis to the first 72 hours to prevent temporal leakage.
Working within computational limits by analyzing a subset of ICU stays.
Using SQLite for efficient relational integration.
The problem is measurable using available variables, and the scope is appropriately 
constrained for a milestone phase focused on database design and exploratory analysis.
