## What evaluation metrics did I choose and why are they appropriate?

### Primary Metric: Recall (Sensitivity) for the Died class
**Why:** In ICU mortality prediction, a false negative (predicting a patient will survive when they will actually die) is far more dangerous than a false positive (predicting death when the patient survives). Missing a high-risk patient means no intervention is triggered. Therefore, maximising recall — catching as many true deaths as possible — is the clinical priority.

**Constraint:** Recall ≥ 0.80 (must catch at least 80% of patients who will die)

### Secondary Metric: Precision with Floor Constraint
**Why:** Optimising recall alone produces a degenerate model that predicts death for everyone — recall = 1.0 but precision ≈ 0.12 (the base rate). This is clinically useless. A precision floor of ≥ 0.40 ensures that when the model flags a patient as high-risk, it is correct at least 40% of the time — acceptable for a screening tool that triggers clinical review rather than direct intervention.

**Custom scorer used:**
```python
def recall_with_precision_floor(y_true, y_pred, precision_floor=0.40):
    if precision_score(y_true, y_pred) < precision_floor:
        return 0
    return recall_score(y_true, y_pred)
```

### Supporting Metrics
- **ROC-AUC:** Measures overall discriminative ability across all thresholds. Score of 0.922 indicates strong separation between survivors and non-survivors.
- **PR-AUC (Average Precision):** More informative than ROC-AUC for imbalanced datasets. Focuses on performance on the minority (death) class.
- **F1-Score:** Harmonic mean of precision and recall. Used for threshold selection after the recall/precision constraints are met.
- **Confusion Matrix:** Visual summary of true positives, false positives, true negatives, false negatives at the selected threshold.

### Why not Accuracy?
With 88% survivors, a model that always predicts survival achieves 88% accuracy — this is misleading. Accuracy is an inappropriate metric for imbalanced clinical datasets.

## What does feature importance suggest?

Feature importance was computed using **permutation importance** on the HGB pipeline applied to the held-out test set. Permutation importance shuffles each feature's values and measures the drop in accuracy — features that cause large drops when shuffled are more important.

### Top findings:
- **Lactate (mean, max):** Consistently the most important feature. Elevated lactate is a direct marker of tissue hypoperfusion and metabolic stress — clinically expected to be the strongest predictor of ICU mortality.
- **Creatinine (mean, max):** Second most important group. Elevated creatinine indicates acute kidney injury, a common and serious complication in critically ill patients.
- **MAP (mean, min):** Mean arterial pressure reflects haemodynamic stability. Low MAP indicates shock — a life-threatening condition.
- **Age:** Older patients have higher mortality risk across all ICU conditions.
- **SpO2 (min):** Minimum oxygen saturation captures the worst hypoxic episode in the 72h window — more predictive than mean SpO2.
- **Urine output:** Low urine output (oliguria) is a marker of renal failure and poor perfusion.
- **BUN:** Elevated blood urea nitrogen reflects kidney dysfunction and protein catabolism.

### Clinical interpretation:
The model has learned clinically meaningful patterns. The top features align precisely with established clinical scoring systems (SOFA, APACHE II, SAPS) which also emphasise organ dysfunction markers — lactate, creatinine, MAP, urine output. This provides confidence that the model is learning genuine clinical signal rather than spurious correlations.

### Low importance features:
- Demographic features (gender, race) had relatively low permutation importance compared to physiological markers, suggesting the model appropriately prioritises clinical state over demographics.
